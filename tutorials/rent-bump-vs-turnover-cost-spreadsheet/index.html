Rent Bump vs Turnover Cost Spreadsheet: Keep, Vacancy Days, Make-Ready and Break-Even Increase (Excel & Google Sheets)

Updated 2026-10-15 · Sheet & Flow

Short on time? The Lease Renewal Tracker ($12) is the finished, tested version of what this tutorial builds.

Setting a renewal rent is not just "what will the market bear." A larger bump that pushes a good tenant out can cost more in vacant days, cleaning, paint, repairs and leasing fees than you gain from the higher new rent — over a full year.

This tutorial builds a one-tab keep-vs-turnover sheet. You type current rent, proposed renewal rent, market rent for a replacement tenant, expected vacant days and the cash costs of a turnover. The sheet calculates the rent bump, 12-month income if they renew, total turnover cost, 12-month net if they leave, whether renewing wins, the break-even renewal increase and how many months of the rent gap would be needed to recover a turnover. It works the same in Excel, Google Sheets and LibreOffice. We tested every formula below by building a workbook with these exact formulas, recalculating it in LibreOffice, and comparing every cell to the worked example and to an independent Python calculation (plus dozens of randomized renewals).

1. The Decision tab (inputs)

One sheet is enough. Type the yellow cells; the rest calculate.

A B
1 Current monthly rent 1450
2 Proposed renewal rent 1520
3 Market rent (new tenant) 1580
4 Expected vacant days if they leave 40
5 Make-ready (clean, paint, repairs) 1100
6 Leasing / placement fee 790
7 Other turnover costs (listing, screening, your time) 250

Current and proposed are the same numbers you track on a lease renewal list (the SAMPLE lease for Oak St Duplex 1A uses $1,450 → $1,520). Market rent is what you honestly expect a replacement tenant to pay on day one — not a wishful listing price. Vacant days should include notice lag, make-ready time and days on market. Put 0 in any cost line you aren't using.

2. The calculated cells

B8:   =IF(OR(B1="",B2=""),"",B2-B1)
B9:   =IF(OR(B1="",B2="",B1=0),"",(B2-B1)/B1)
B10:  =IF(B2="","",12*B2)
B11:  =IF(OR(B3="",B4=""),"",B3*12/365*B4)
B12:  =SUM(B5:B7)
B13:  =IF(OR(B11="",B12=""),"",B11+B12)
B14:  =IF(OR(B3="",B13=""),"",12*B3-B13)
B15:  =IF(OR(B10="",B14=""),"",B10-B14)
B16:  =IF(OR(B1="",B1=0,B14=""),"",B14/(12*B1)-1)
B17:  =IF(B15="","",IF(B15>=0,"Renewing at this rent earns more over 12 months","Turning over earns more over 12 months (if vacancy estimate holds)"))
B18:  =IF(OR(B3="",B2="",B3<=B2,B13=""),"",B13/(B3-B2))

Label them in column A: Bump $, Bump %, 12-month income if they renew, Lost rent while vacant, Cash turnover costs, Total turnover cost, 12-month net if they leave, Renewal advantage, Break-even renewal increase, Verdict, Months to recover turnover via rent gap.

What each one does:

  • B8 / B9: proposed minus current, and that difference as a fraction of current. Format B9 as a percentage. These match the bump columns on a lease renewal tracker.
  • B10: twelve months of the proposed rent if the tenant stays (no vacancy).
  • B11: proration of market rent for the vacant days (market × 12 / 365 × days). Using market rent (not current) prices the empty days at what you could have collected from a new lease.
  • B12 / B13: cash make-ready and leasing costs, then cash plus lost rent = total turnover cost.
  • B14: a full year at market rent, minus the total turnover cost. That is the 12-month net if they leave and you re-lease at market after the vacancy.
  • B15: renew income minus turn net. Positive means renewing wins on these assumptions.
  • B16: the renewal increase (as a fraction of current rent) that would make 12-month renew income equal the turn net. It can be negative when turnover is expensive — meaning even a flat renewal still beats turning the unit.
  • B17: plain-language verdict from the sign of B15.
  • B18: if market is above proposed, how many months of that monthly gap would it take to earn back the total turnover cost. Blank when market is not higher than proposed (there is no gap to "earn back" against).

Optional: vacant days from a move-out and re-lease date

If you already know the expected move-out and the day a new lease would start:

B4 (from dates in C4 and D4):  =IF(OR(C4="",D4=""),"",D4-C4)

Put the move-out date in C4 and the new lease start in D4, and leave B4 as this formula instead of a typed day count.

3. Notice rules before you send the bump

This sheet is economics, not a notice calendar. Your lease and state law still set how much written notice a rent increase needs. For example, Colorado C.R.S. 38-12-701 requires at least 60 days' written notice to raise rent in a residential tenancy with no written agreement; other tenancies follow the lease or other statutes. Check yours before you mail or email an offer. Pair this decision sheet with a lease renewal tracker for expiration windows and send-by dates.

Lost rent while vacant is an economic cost in this comparison. For tax treatment of vacant rental property (you generally cannot deduct the lost income itself, only allowed expenses while it is held for rent), see IRS Publication 527. This tutorial is not tax advice.

Worked example

Oak St Duplex 1A style numbers: current rent $1,450, proposed renewal $1,520 (the same bump as the Lease Renewal Tracker SAMPLE for that unit). Market rent for a replacement $1,580. Expected vacant days 40. Make-ready $1,100, leasing fee $790, other costs $250.

Field Value
Bump $ 70.00
Bump % 4.83%
12-month income if they renew 18240.00
Lost rent while vacant 2077.81
Cash turnover costs 2140.00
Total turnover cost 4217.81
12-month net if they leave 14742.19
Renewal advantage 3497.81
Break-even renewal increase -15.27%
Verdict Renewing at this rent earns more over 12 months
Months to recover turnover via rent gap 70.3

What the example shows:

  1. The bump itself is modest. $1,520 − $1,450 = $70, or 4.83%.
  2. Turnover is expensive even when market is only $60 above proposed. Lost rent ≈ $2,077.81 plus $2,140 cash = about $4,217.81 of total turnover cost.
  3. Renewing wins by about $3,497.81 over 12 months ($18,240 renew income vs about $14,742.19 turn net).
  4. Break-even increase is negative (−15.27%). On these vacancy and cost assumptions, even a flat renewal still beats turning the unit — the sheet is telling you not to chase a small market premium.
  5. The rent gap would take ~70.3 months to "earn back" a turnover. $4,217.81 ÷ ($1,580 − $1,520) ≈ 70.3 months of the $60 gap.

A second case, same current rent, where turning over wins: proposed only $1,470, market $1,700, vacant days 14, cash turnover costs $800 (put 800 in B5 and 0 in B6/B7). Renew 12-month income = $17,640. Turn net ≈ $18,817.53. Renewal advantage ≈ −$1,177.53 → verdict Turning over earns more over 12 months (if vacancy estimate holds).

Common mistakes

  • Optimistic vacant days. Fourteen days assumes the unit shows clean the day they leave and a signed lease starts immediately. Add make-ready and advertising time.
  • Listing price as "market." Use what comparable units actually leased for recently, not the ask.
  • Forgetting your own time. Put an hours × rate line into B7 if you self-manage.
  • Comparing one month instead of twelve. A single month of higher rent never covers a multi-thousand-dollar turnover.
  • Sending an increase without checking notice rules. Run the economics here, then use your lease / state notice period before you send anything.
